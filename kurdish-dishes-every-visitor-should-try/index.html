What to Eat in Kurdistan Before Anyone Hands You Hummus

A local guide to traditional Kurdish dishes, from sour dolma in Erbil to kubba hamusta in Slemani, with real prices and where to find them.

The short answer is dolma, kubba hamusta, a Slemani biryani, bread straight off the saj, and one long breakfast of kahi with qaymar. I've walked maybe a dozen visiting friends through this exact list, and the first attempt, back in October 2022, went sideways. I ordered dolma for two at a lunch spot behind Erbil's Qaysari bazaar and a tray for six arrived, 9,000 dinars, and the owner stood there until we finished most of it. Here's my position after years of eating across this region... the best Kurdish food is not in hotel restaurants or the new fusion places on Gulan Street. It's in lunch-only rooms with plastic tablecloths that sell out by 2pm.

There's a misconception worth killing early. Visitors land expecting Levantine mezze, hummus and falafel and small plates, because that's what "Middle Eastern food" means abroad. Kurdish cooking is a different animal entirely: sour where Lebanese food is bright, built on rice and slow broth rather than dips, and eaten fast at lunch rather than late at night. And with the Kurdistan Regional Government reporting close to 7 million tourist visits in 2023 (Kurdistan Regional Government), a lot of people are about to order the wrong thing.

Dolma Is the Test, So Order It Right

Kurdish dolma, called yaprax here, is the dish locals judge a kitchen by. Vine leaves, onions, peppers and chard get stuffed with rice and lamb, stacked in a pot, and cooked upside down under flatbread with a sour tamarind edge that you won't find in the Turkish or Greek versions. It's a Friday family ritual, which means the restaurant version on a Tuesday is the real measure of a cook.

Go at lunch, never dinner. The good pots are made once, in the morning, and when they're gone they're gone. In my experience the spots within a five minute walk of the Qaysari bazaar in Erbil are the safest bet for a first-timer.

A quick order list with honest prices:

  • Dolma plate for one: 7,000 to 10,000 IQD, roughly $5 to $7
  • Kubba hamusta bowl in Slemani: around 6,000 IQD
  • Kahi with qaymar breakfast: 4,000 to 6,000 IQD
  • Full kebab spread with bread and tea: 10,000 to 14,000 IQD

Slemani Runs on Sour Soup and Cheap Biryani

Sulaymaniyah eats differently than Erbil, and its two flags are kubba hamusta and biryani. Kubba hamusta is a pale green, lemony broth of chard and celery carrying soft rice-flour dumplings stuffed with minced meat. Sounds gentle. It's actually the most addictive thing in the region, and locals argue about the correct sourness the way Italians argue about carbonara.

When I tested three biryani places around Slemani's Grand Bazaar last spring, the cheapest one won by a mile. Kurdish biryani is its own thing, yellow rice with noodles, peas, potato, chicken and raisins, closer to a festive pilaf than anything Indian. At 6,000 dinars a plate it might be the best value meal in Iraq.

If you only have one day in Slemani, eat kubba hamusta at lunch and biryani is your backup. Both peak between noon and 2pm.

Breakfast and the Tea House Are Not Optional

Kurdish breakfast deserves a slow morning. The centerpiece is kahi with qaymar, a flaky layered pastry drowned in syrup and topped with thick buffalo cream, usually with honey, cheese and hot flatbread on the side. So yes, it's dessert at 8am. Nobody here apologizes for that.

Then there's tea. The chaikhana, the tea house, is where business, gossip and politics actually happen. Machko, the tea house built into the wall of Erbil's citadel, has been pouring dark sweet tea since 1940, and the citadel above it was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2014 (UNESCO World Heritage Centre). Tea costs about 500 dinars. Sit for an hour, nobody will rush you.

The Brave Orders, From Sar u Pe to Bean Stew

Fasolia w brinj, white bean stew ladled over rice, is the everyday lunch of the entire region and the dish I'd hand any nervous eater first. Shifta, thin spiced meat patties, are the home-cooking equivalent of a hug. And the kebab houses along Erbil's Iskan street do a charcoal lamb kebab that embarrasses most of what gets grilled in Istanbul.

Then there's sar u pe, sheep's head and trotters simmered overnight into a broth people line up for at dawn. I'll be honest with you, the first time I ordered it I mostly did it to impress an uncle, and I spent ten minutes quietly moving things around the bowl while he watched me like a hawk. My friend Aram swears it cures anything... I'm not 100% sure it's for everyone, and that's fine. The broth alone, with bread torn into it, is genuinely great even if you never make eye contact with the rest.

Skip the shiny fusion restaurants on your first trip. They're fine, some are good, but they're translating a cuisine you can hear in its own accent two streets away for a third of the price.

Do this on your first morning: walk into any crowded lunch room before 1pm, point at whatever pot is emptiest, and order that. An emptying pot at noon is the most reliable restaurant review in Kurdistan, better than any app, and it will feed you for about $5.

Frequently asked questions

What is the most famous Kurdish dish?

Dolma, called yaprax locally, is the dish Kurds treat as their signature. Vine leaves and vegetables are stuffed with rice and lamb and cooked with a sour tamarind edge. Kubba hamusta, a lemony dumpling soup, is the pride of Sulaymaniyah.

Is Kurdish food spicy?

No, heat is rare. The dominant flavors are sour and savory, built on tamarind, sumac, pomegranate and slow-cooked broth. Chili usually appears only as an optional pickle or sauce on the side.

How much does a meal cost in Erbil or Sulaymaniyah?

A full plate at a local lunch spot runs 6,000 to 12,000 Iraqi dinars, roughly $4 to $9, with bread and tea often included. Breakfast of kahi with qaymar costs about 4,000 to 6,000 dinars.
