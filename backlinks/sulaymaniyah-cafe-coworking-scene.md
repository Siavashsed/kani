**Title:** Has anyone else noticed cafes in Sulaymaniyah doubling as offices? Curious how people handle power cuts and Wi-Fi

I've been working remotely from Sulaymaniyah on and off, and something has shifted. Cafes are quietly doing the job coworking spaces were supposed to do.

The reason is mostly practical. Power cuts are the big one. A few months ago my rented desk went dark at 2:40 pm in the middle of a client call. I walked about eight minutes to a cafe off Salim Street, ordered a $2 latte, and finished the call on generator-backed Wi-Fi. That one afternoon changed how I think about where to work.

The economics help too. A latte costs a fraction of a coworking membership, nobody minds if you stay a couple of hours, and the better cafes already have backup power and decent connections because their own business depends on it. You also get a lot of freelancers, students and small business owners in the same room, which is useful on its own.

The trade-offs are real: noise at peak hours, no private space for sensitive calls, and not every cafe is laptop-friendly.

For those working in Slemani, Erbil or elsewhere in Iraq, what do you use as your backup when the power or internet fails? Any cafes you'd recommend, or do you stick with a proper coworking space?

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Where can you work remotely in Sulaymaniyah, Iraq: a coworking space or a cafe?

For most people in Sulaymaniyah right now, a good cafe is the more practical choice, with a few caveats.

The deciding factor is infrastructure. Power cuts are common across the Kurdistan Region, and a workspace that loses electricity or internet is useless however nice it looks. Many of the better cafes run generators and reliable connections because their own business depends on them. That makes them more dependable than some coworking spaces, which can be slower to invest in backup systems.

Cost is the second factor. A coffee costs a couple of dollars, while a coworking membership is a recurring monthly commitment. If you work irregular hours, travel between Erbil and Slemani, or are just testing the city, a cafe lets you stay flexible. Cafes also tend to attract freelancers, students and small business owners, so you meet useful people without paying for a membership.

Where cafes fall short:

1. Noise during lunch and evening peaks.
2. Little privacy for confidential calls or client meetings.
3. Unwritten etiquette. Buy something regularly, avoid holding a table for hours at busy times, and keep calls short.
4. No guaranteed seat, desk storage or meeting rooms.

A coworking space makes more sense if you need a fixed desk, a business address, a quiet room for calls, or a team of several people working together.

A sensible approach is to use both: a cafe as your default and a day pass or meeting room when you need real privacy. Test two or three cafes at different hours before settling on one, and ask about generator coverage and Wi-Fi speed.
